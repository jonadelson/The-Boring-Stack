Accessibility: The Quality You Get For Free, Until You Don't

Lesson 9 · html · ~8 min

Accessibility has a reputation as a compliance chore bolted on at the end. The reality is closer to the opposite: on the boring stack, most of it is the default, and inaccessibility is something teams build, expensively, by fighting the platform. This lesson is partly new material and partly a revelation about lessons you've already read — because without saying so, this site has been doing accessibility work since lesson 2.

Who it's for (it's you)

The blind user with a screen reader is real and matters. But disability is a spectrum with three bands — permanent (blindness), temporary (eye surgery, a broken arm), and situational (bright sunlight, a phone held one-handed on a train, a video watched muted). Everyone occupies the third band regularly. Captions were built for deaf users; most caption-hours today happen in muted feeds. Keyboard navigation was built for motor impairments; its heaviest users are developers with a broken trackpad and power users who hate the mouse. Build for the edge and the middle benefits — that's not charity, it's engineering.

How a screen reader sees this page

Assistive technology doesn't read your pixels; it reads the browser's accessibility tree — a structured outline derived from your markup. Screen reader users don't sit through pages top to bottom: they jump landmark to landmark (<main>, <nav>), skim heading to heading, and pull up lists of all links. Which means the semantic HTML from lesson 2 wasn't stylistic preference — it was the data structure assistive tech navigates by. A div-soup page presents to that tree as one undifferentiated blob: technically present, practically unusable.

Two consequences worth internalizing. Headings are an outline, so their levels must nest — jumping from h1 to h4 because the h4 "looked right" breaks navigation; if the size is wrong, fix it in CSS. And link text is read out of context in those link lists — a page of "click here" and "read more" becomes a menu of identical mystery doors. Name the destination: "read the full roadmap."

Links go places, buttons do things

The most common accessibility sin in framework code is the clickable <div>. A real <button> is focusable by Tab, activates on Enter and Space, and announces itself as a button — three behaviors a div-with-onClick has to reimplement and almost never does. The distinction matters too: a link navigates (and supports open-in-new-tab, copy address, all the browser's link affordances); a button acts. Styling a button to look like a link, or vice versa, is fine. Swapping the elements is not.

The keyboard test takes thirty seconds and finds most problems: put down the mouse, Tab through the page. Can you reach every control? Can you see where you are? If focus is invisible, someone "fixed" the focus outline with outline: none and forgot the people it existed for — the modern move is :focus-visible, which shows the ring for keyboard users without flashing it at mouse clicks.

Color is a hint, never the message

Two rules. Contrast: text needs roughly a 4.5:1 ratio against its background — grey-on-grey aesthetics fail real eyes in real sunlight, and free checkers will grade any pair of colors. Redundancy: about 8% of men see red and green differently, so "errors are in red" must also be "errors are marked with a message" — color reinforces, something else carries. (The forms lesson's invalid-field styling pairs the red border with the browser's error text for exactly this reason.)

About ARIA

You'll meet aria-* attributes — a vocabulary for annotating markup with roles and states that HTML can't express. It has an official First Rule, and the boring stack endorses it completely: don't use ARIA when a native element does the job. <button> beats <div role="button" tabindex="0"> plus the keyboard handlers you forgot. ARIA is a repair kit for the gaps — this site's SVG diagram uses role="img" and an aria-label because SVG genuinely needs the help. A page needing lots of ARIA is usually a page that chose the wrong elements.

The boring truth: almost everything in this lesson was already done in lessons 2 through 8 — landmarks and headings (2), labeled inputs and native validation (6), alt text and the labeled SVG (8). That's the point. Accessibility on the boring stack is mostly not undoing what the platform provides. The expensive retrofits happen on rung 5, where teams rebuild links, buttons, and forms from divs and then buy back each native behavior one bug report at a time.

Try it: the visceral version

You're on your phone. Turn on the screen reader — VoiceOver on iOS (Settings → Accessibility), TalkBack on Android — and swipe through this page with your eyes closed. Then try a div-heavy site you know. Five minutes of that teaches more than any checklist, and it turns the accessibility tree from an abstraction into an experience.

Check yourself

Why do heading levels have to nest properly when CSS can make any heading any size?

Headings form the document outline that screen reader users navigate by — skipping levels breaks the structure. Visual size is CSS's job; the level is semantics.

What three things does a real <button> provide that a clickable div doesn't?

Keyboard focusability (Tab), activation via Enter and Space, and announcing itself as a button to assistive tech — all free, all missing from the div until hand-rebuilt.

What's the thirty-second accessibility test, and what does it catch?

Put down the mouse and Tab through the page: unreachable controls, invisible focus (outline: none), and broken ordering — the majority of common failures.

What's the First Rule of ARIA?

Don't use it if a native HTML element or attribute already provides the semantics and behavior — reach for ARIA only to fill genuine gaps, like labeling an inline SVG.
