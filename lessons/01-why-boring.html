Why Boring?

Lesson 1 · philosophy · ~4 min

In 2015 Dan McKinley wrote an essay called "Choose Boring Technology" that gave a name to something experienced engineers had long felt: the exciting new tool is usually the wrong choice, and the old, dull, battle-tested one is usually right.

His core idea is innovation tokens. Imagine your project gets about three. Each token lets you adopt one novel, interesting technology. Spend a token on the thing that makes your project special. Spend the rest of your choices on boring technology — not because boring is good in itself, but because boring means known.

The boring truth: "Boring" doesn't mean bad or old-fashioned. It means the failure modes are well understood. When Postgres breaks at 3am, the answer is on the first page of search results. When this month's hot new database breaks at 3am, you may be the first person to ever see that error.

The capabilities you see, the failures you don't

Every technology choice is a bundle of things it does well (which the documentation advertises) and ways it fails (which you discover in production). New technology dazzles with the first list and hides the second. Mature technology has had its failure list written out in public, over decades, by other people — at their expense, not yours.

That's why "boring" technologies keep winning on merit: Postgres over the newest database, server-rendered HTML over a single-page app, plain CSS over a styling framework, cron over a distributed job queue. Not always — but far more often than the excitement gradient suggests.

What this site is

This site teaches those technologies, and it is built from them. It is hand-written HTML and CSS — no framework, no build step, and only as much JavaScript as has been earned. It will grow — quizzes, then a database, then user accounts — but each layer of complexity gets added only when the current stack genuinely hurts, and the moment it hurts is itself the lesson. You'll see exactly what a database buys you, because you'll feel life without one first.

The curriculum ahead: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, HTTP, SQL and Postgres, forms, cookies and sessions, security, and the operational layer — DNS, TLS, backups — that most tutorials skip.

Check yourself

What is an "innovation token"?

A budget metaphor: a project can only afford a small number of novel technology choices. Spend them deliberately on what makes your project special, and choose well-understood technology for everything else.

What does "boring" actually mean in this context?

Well understood — especially the failure modes. A boring technology is one whose ways of breaking have been documented by decades of other people's production incidents.

How did this quiz reveal its answers without JavaScript?

With the HTML <details> and <summary> elements — the browser provides the tap-to-reveal behavior natively. That's the next lesson.
